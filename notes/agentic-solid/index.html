This document is a map, not a specification. It describes how a set of existing W3C standards and a small number of component specifications compose into Agentic Solid: an architecture in which autonomous software agents act on data their principals control, and in which every agent action is mechanically traceable to a delegating principal through a signed, revocable, scoped chain of standard artifacts. The architecture layers identity and authentication ([[WEBID]], [[SOLID-OIDC]], [[RFC9449]]), access control and authorized query ([[WAC]], [[ACP]], [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]]), policy and delegation ([[ODRL-MODEL]], [[ODRL-DELEGATION]]), credentials and integrity ([[VC-DATA-MODEL-2.0]], [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]], [[AGENT-AUTHZ]]), agent communication and discovery ([[A2A]], [[ANP]], [[AGORA]]), and accountability ([[PROV-O]], [[AAR-DESIGN]]) — with data federations ([[FED-VOCAB]]) as the cross-cutting interoperability plane. Each layer is defined normatively by its own specification; this document defines none of them. It states the architecture's single organising claim, locates each component, frames staged conformance profiles by reference, records an honest implementation-maturity inventory, and explains the architecture's relationship to the existing Solid specifications it builds on and does not replace.

This document is an unofficial editor's draft shaped as a W3C Solid Community Group Note. It is not an adopted Solid Community Group work item, has no standing in the Solid CG or any other W3C group, and is not a W3C Standard nor on the W3C standards track. Proposing it to the Solid Community Group is a deliberate later step, taken only after the component specifications it maps have themselves been contributed.

This document is entirely informative. It contains no normative requirements of its own; all conformance language lives in the component specifications it references ().

Introduction

Autonomous software agents increasingly read, write, negotiate over, and act on data. Built on today's default substrate — scraped pages, proprietary APIs, unsigned text between black boxes — an agent's actions are unattributable, its authority is implicit, and responsibility for its mistakes is deniable. The case for a different substrate, assembled almost entirely from existing W3C Recommendations, is made at length in the companion vision paper, The Accountable Web of Agents [[ACCOUNTABLE-AGENTS]]; this document does not restate that case. It does the complementary job: for a reader arriving from a standards body, it shows how the pieces cohere — which specification answers which question, where the layer boundaries are, what a conforming implementation of each stage looks like, and what is real today versus drafted versus merely proposed.

Reading guide:

  • points at (and does not repeat) the worked end-to-end scenario.
  • frames what "Agentic-Solid conformant" means, in stages, by reference to the component specifications.
  • is the honest inventory: implemented, drafted, or proposed, per component.
  • explains why this architecture extends the existing Solid specifications and changes none of them.
  • and index the security and privacy analyses that live in each component, and add the concerns that only the composition raises.

The accountability thesis

Many stacks give agents identity; several give them tools; a few give them policies. The property that distinguishes this architecture — its single load-bearing claim — is:

Every action taken by an autonomous agent is mechanically traceable to a delegating principal, through a chain of signed, revocable, scoped artifacts, each of which is a standard, independently verifiable document.

"Mechanically traceable" decomposes into four properties, each supplied by a distinct layer and each checkable without trusting the party being audited:

  1. Attributable. The acting agent is a first-class identity — a WebID [[WEBID]] of its own, authenticated per request by proof-of-possession ([[SOLID-OIDC]], [[RFC9449]]) — and every artifact it produces carries [[PROV-O]] attribution naming the agent, the activity, and, via prov:actedOnBehalfOf, the principal it represents. Holding a credential is not authority; being the authenticated agent the credential names is.
  2. Authorized. The agent's mandate is an explicit delegation chain of ODRL policies — a root Agreement from the principal, plus zero or more delegated hops — evaluated fail-closed for scope, expiry, prohibitions, duties, and delegation depth per the ODRL Agent-Delegation Profile [[ODRL-DELEGATION]]. Absence of a grant is a denial, never a default.
  3. Bounded and revocable. Each hop of the chain is issued as an AgentAuthorizationCredential [[AGENT-AUTHZ]]: a Verifiable Credential 2.0 [[VC-DATA-MODEL-2.0]] signed by that hop's delegator with a Data Integrity proof [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]], content-bound to its policy, and carrying a revocation status entry ([[VC-BITSTRING-STATUS-LIST]] mapped bidirectionally to the profile's odrld:Revocation). A principal can withdraw a mandate, and a verifier that cannot reach status information resolves closed.
  4. Verifiable offline. Because proofs are over the canonicalised graph [[RDF-CANON]] rather than a transport session, any downstream holder — including an auditor years later — can verify integrity, issuer binding, and the whole chain without contacting the systems that produced it.

The honest boundary, stated here once because every component restates it in its own terms: the architecture provides accountability, not prevention. Cryptography enforces the authenticity of the trail — that the mandate was signed by its issuer, that the artifact is intact, that the actor is who the transport authenticated. Whether an agent's use of validly granted access respected the agreement's purpose is proved afterwards, from the trail, and enforced legally — a deliberate design position: the alternative, purely technical usage enforcement, is DRM, which fails and centralises ([[ACCOUNTABLE-AGENTS]] §4; [[AAR-DESIGN]] §5). What the architecture guarantees is that "the AI did it" is never an answer: the AI did it on the owner's signed, scoped, revocable authority, and the trail proves exactly which authority that was.

The layered architecture

Six layers, plus one cross-cutting plane. Each subsection names the layer's job in one sentence, the specification(s) that define it normatively, and how it connects to the layers beside it. Nothing in this section is normative; the pointers are.

L1 — Identity and authentication

Job: one uniform identity mechanism for humans, organisations, and agents, with per-request proof-of-possession authentication. Normative: [[SOLID-PROTOCOL]], [[WEBID]], [[SOLID-OIDC]], [[RFC9449]].

Every actor holds a WebID — an HTTP URI dereferencing to a profile document. Crucially for everything above, an agent's WebID is the same kind of thing as a person's: the principal's profile links to the agent's own WebID (an agent pointer; see the descriptor conventions of [[SOLID-AGENT-CARD-IMPL]]), so authorization (L2/L3), credentials (L4), and attribution (L6) can all name agents and principals with the same identifier type. Authentication is [[SOLID-OIDC]] with DPoP-bound tokens [[RFC9449]]: the storage server learns, per request, a proof-of-possession-authenticated WebID — the ground fact the accountability chain's Phase-D actor check anchors to ([[AGENT-AUTHZ]], Verification).

L2 — Access control and authorized query

Job: declarative, server-enforced access control over pod resources, and a query interface that can never leak past it. Normative: [[WAC]], [[ACP]]; [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]] for the query interface.

Authorization stays exactly what Solid already defines: [[WAC]] or [[ACP]] rules evaluated by the storage server. The architecture adds one component at this layer: Access-Controlled SPARQL Query over a Solid Pod [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]], a read-only SPARQL 1.1 Protocol endpoint whose queried dataset is restricted — one named graph per readable RDF resource, empty default graph, restriction applied before evaluation — to exactly the subset of the pod the authenticated agent may read, with existence-non-disclosure invariants. It is a new read interface over the existing authorization model, not a new authorization model: an agent asking questions (L5) gets answers computed under the same [[WAC]]/[[ACP]] rules that would govern resource-by-resource GETs.

L3 — Policy and delegation

Job: express what an agent is mandated to do — scope, duties, prohibitions, expiry, and how far it may re-delegate — as machine-readable agreements. Normative: [[ODRL-MODEL]], [[ODRL-VOCAB]]; the ODRL Agent-Delegation Profile [[ODRL-DELEGATION]] for chain semantics.

ODRL 2.2 supplies policies and, critically, Agreements — policies with an identified assigner and assignee: machine-readable contracts. What ODRL 2.2 leaves unspecified is the chain: who allowed this agent to allow that agent, how deep, revocable how. The Agent-Delegation Profile pins those semantics fail-closed — conservative subset semantics (a delegate's effective permissions can never exceed the delegator's), explicit bounded delegation authority via odrl:grantUse, an upstream authority link (odrld:delegatedUnder, a subproperty of prov:wasDerivedFrom — the hook into L6), depth bounds defaulting to no re-delegation, and revocation. The profile's evaluator deliberately checks validity and scope given an authenticated chain; proving who authored each hop is L4's job — the two layers are designed as halves of one verification ([[AGENT-AUTHZ]], Introduction).

L4 — Credentials and integrity

Job: make every artifact that carries authority — and any data that must survive copying — signed, tamper-evident, issuer-bound, and revocable. Normative: [[VC-DATA-MODEL-2.0]], [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]], [[VC-DI-EDDSA]], [[VC-DI-ECDSA]], [[RDF-CANON]], [[VC-BITSTRING-STATUS-LIST]]; the Agent Authorization Credentials note [[AGENT-AUTHZ]] for the mandate credential.

Data Integrity proofs sign the canonicalised graph, so signed data remains queryable, mergeable Linked Data, verifiable offline at any downstream hop. The architecture's specific contribution at this layer is the AgentAuthorizationCredential [[AGENT-AUTHZ]]: one credential per delegation hop, signed by that hop's delegator, content-binding the hop's ODRL policy (bare policy references are rejected), carrying a status entry, and verified by a four-phase, fail-closed algorithm — credential gates → cross-binding (credential ↔ policy ↔ WebIDs) → status ∪ revocation → the L3 chain evaluation. The layering rule the note makes explicit governs the whole stack: credential verification establishes authenticity and currency of the artifacts; policy evaluation establishes authorization of the request; both, in that order, fail-closed. The same credential machinery signs federation memberships (the plane below) and any other claim the architecture needs to travel.

L5 — Agent communication and discovery

Job: let agents find each other from a WebID and converse in typed, validated RDF — using natural language only to negotiate what is not yet formalised. Normative/reference: [[A2A]], [[ANP]], [[AGORA]]; reference implementations [[SOLID-AGENT-CARD-IMPL]], [[SOLID-A2A-IMPL]].

Discovery: a WebID profile advertises the agent representing its owner; the descriptor is dual-published — an [[A2A]] Agent Card (plain JSON, for the industry toolchain) and an [[ANP]]-aligned RDF description (for the Linked Data toolchain) — so an agent is findable and self-describing before any conversation starts. Conversation: following the AGORA model [[AGORA]], agents may negotiate in natural language once, then crystallise the routine as a hash-pinned protocol document whose body is a SHACL shape [[SHACL]], content-addressed and pod-hosted; thereafter exchange is SHACL-validated RDF with no LLM inference on the hot path, and a no-silent-downgrade rule prevents an agent being talked back into ambiguous prose on security-bearing steps. [[A2A]] remains the maximum-reach first-contact envelope; [[MCP]] sits adjacent as the tool seam between an LLM host and a pod — an edge integration, not an agent-to-agent layer. Intents crossing this layer are what L3 evaluates and L6 records.

L6 — Accountability and audit

Job: record who made what, how, acting for whom, under which mandate — and make the audit a mechanical walk. Normative: [[PROV-O]]; composition designed in [[AAR-DESIGN]].

Every agent-generated resource carries (or links to) a [[PROV-O]] record: the entity, the generating activity, the software agent (by WebID), and — prov:actedOnBehalfOf — the responsible principal, with a link to the governing agreement. Because L3's authority edges are declared subproperties of prov:wasDerivedFrom, and L4's verifier emits PROV attribution for the chains it validates, the delegation structure itself is visible to generic PROV consumers. The reference accountable-agent runtime [[AAR-DESIGN]] specifies the composed artifact — an on-pod trace container from which an auditor answers, mechanically: who authorized this action, under what policy, and was it in scope? The auditor needs nothing but read access to the trace; that is the point.

Cross-cutting: data federations

Job: make independent implementations actually interoperable — shared sector vocabularies and shapes, self-description, membership with lifecycle and trust, and schema migration without a flag day. Reference: [[FED-VOCAB]] (the fedapp:/fedreg: vocabularies and sector ontologies), [[FED-REGISTRY-IMPL]], [[FED-TRUST-IMPL]].

Federations are a plane, not a layer, because they touch several layers at once: an app or agent self-describes (in its Client Identifier Document) which sectors it operates in, which SHACL shapes it consumes and produces, and which access modes it requests (fedapp: — read at L2 consent time and L5 discovery time); a registry asserts membership as its own lifecycle-managed record — never trusting self-assertion (fedreg:); membership assertions are optionally backed by Verifiable Credentials with delegation chains to a trust anchor (L4, via [[FED-TRUST-IMPL]]); and storage advertises which specification versions it accepts, so apps, pods, and servers migrate on independent clocks. The sector pattern is deliberately repeatable: a new domain mints a sector ontology that imports the shared core, reuses established vocabularies, and registers under the sector namespace — the futures sector (fut:, the participatory-democracy domain of the unite platform design) is the worked recent example, built with no changes to the federation machinery. Deliberately, the federation vocabularies are not yet proposed for standardisation: that waits for implementation experience from a second independent consumer ().

The worked scenario (by reference)

The architecture's layers are exercised end to end by one worked scenario — a principal issues her agent a mandate; the agent discovers a counterparty, negotiates a data-sharing agreement within that mandate, receives scoped access, acts; a year later an auditor walks the trail from an offending artifact back to the signed authority it was produced under. The narrative form is [[ACCOUNTABLE-AGENTS]] §4; the same scenario walked API-call-by-API-call against the current component implementations, including the honest gap list between design and code, is [[AAR-DESIGN]] (SCENARIO.md). This document deliberately does not restate it.

Conformance profiles

This document defines no conformance requirements. Conformance is defined by the component specifications; what this section defines is a shared vocabulary of profiles — named bundles of component-specification conformance — so that "Agentic-Solid conformant" is a precise, staged claim rather than a vibe. The stages are cumulative: each includes all requirements of the stages below it. An implementation claims a stage for a role; the roles are:

  • Server — a Solid storage server;
  • Principal tooling — software with which a person or organisation issues and revokes mandates;
  • Agent — the autonomous actor;
  • Verifier — any party checking a chain before granting access or during audit (servers, counterparty agents, auditors);
  • Registry — a federation membership authority.

AS-0 — Solid base

Exactly today's Solid, unmodified. Server: conforms to [[SOLID-PROTOCOL]] with [[WAC]] or [[ACP]] and [[SOLID-OIDC]]. Agent / principal tooling: authenticates with DPoP-bound Solid-OIDC [[RFC9449]]; each agent holds its own WebID [[WEBID]], linked from its principal's profile. A server MAY additionally implement the access-controlled query interface [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]] — an orthogonal server capability, valuable at every stage.

AS-1 — Attributed and verifiable data

AS-0, plus: Agent: attaches [[PROV-O]] attribution (agent WebID, activity, prov:actedOnBehalfOf the principal) to every resource it generates. Principal tooling / agent: artifacts that must survive copying carry Data Integrity proofs conforming to [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]] over [[RDF-CANON]] (cryptosuites [[VC-DI-EDDSA]] or [[VC-DI-ECDSA]]). Verifier: verifies such proofs per [[VC-DATA-MODEL-2.0]] / [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]].

AS-2 — Mandated agents (the accountability core)

AS-1, plus: Principal tooling: issues mandates as AgentAuthorizationCredentials conforming to [[AGENT-AUTHZ]] (one credential per hop, policy content-bound, status entry per [[VC-BITSTRING-STATUS-LIST]]), over policies conforming to the ODRL Agent-Delegation Profile [[ODRL-DELEGATION]] (which builds on [[ODRL-MODEL]] / [[ODRL-VOCAB]]); and can revoke them. Agent: acts only within a chain it can present; declares odrld:delegatedUnder on any hop it issues. Verifier: implements the full four-phase verification of [[AGENT-AUTHZ]] — credential gates, cross-binding, status ∪ revocation, then the fail-closed chain evaluation of [[ODRL-DELEGATION]] — in that order, and additionally checks that the transport-authenticated WebID (AS-0) is the chain's leaf delegate. AS-2 is the stage at which the accountability thesis holds in full.

AS-3 — Negotiating agents

AS-2, plus: Agent: publishes a discovery descriptor (an [[A2A]] Agent Card and an RDF agent description, linked from the owner's WebID profile — the conventions of [[SOLID-AGENT-CARD-IMPL]]); supports the NL→RDF upgrade — hash-pinned, SHACL-bodied protocol documents with the no-silent-downgrade rule for security-bearing steps (the codec of [[SOLID-A2A-IMPL]], following [[AGORA]]) — and validates inbound typed exchange against the pinned shape [[SHACL]]. Note: the descriptor and protocol-document conventions are currently defined by their reference implementations, not by standalone specifications (); AS-3 will firm up as those conventions are contributed to the [[A2A]] project and/or a CG.

AS-4 — Federated

AS-3, plus: Agent (and any app): self-describes with fedapp: metadata in its Client Identifier Document (sectors, shapes consumed/produced, access modes) [[FED-VOCAB]]. Registry: asserts membership as fedreg:Membership records with lifecycle status, treating self-assertion as non-membership; optionally backs assertions with Verifiable Credentials and delegation chains to a trust anchor ([[FED-TRUST-IMPL]], which composes the AS-2 machinery). Server: advertises accepted specification versions (fedreg:StorageDescription) so schema migration needs no flag day. Verifier: checks membership through the registry (or its credentials), never through self-assertion.

Relationship to the existing Solid specifications

This architecture extends Solid; it replaces nothing. Concretely:

  • The base is unmodified. AS-0 () is exactly [[SOLID-PROTOCOL]] + [[WAC]]/[[ACP]] + [[SOLID-OIDC]] as published by the Solid Community Group. No component here changes a requirement of any of them; an existing conforming Solid server is already an AS-0 server.
  • WAC/ACP remain the authorization model. [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]] adds a read interface under the existing model, with non-disclosure invariants ensuring it can never answer beyond what resource-level access control allows. The ODRL layer (L3) sits above access control as the mandate and agreement layer: today it is evaluated client-side by verifiers, and the pod server continues to enforce only WAC/ACP. Server-side ODRL enforcement beside WAC is explicitly a proposed, separately-reviewed future step () — precisely because it would touch the base's authorization path.
  • Identity stays WebID + Solid-OIDC. Agents get no new identity mechanism; they get the existing one, plus profile-level linkage to their principals. DPoP [[RFC9449]] is already the Solid-OIDC token binding.
  • Nothing requires pods to change shape. Credentials, policies, protocol documents, and traces are ordinary pod resources under ordinary access control.
  • The standards path runs through the existing venues. The base's standards-track home is the W3C Linked Web Storage Working Group [[LWS-WG]]; the component specifications target the venues that own their layers — the Solid CG ([[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]], and eventually this document), the ODRL CG ([[ODRL-DELEGATION]]), the W3C Credentials CG ([[AGENT-AUTHZ]]), and the Linux Foundation A2A project (the protocol-document extension). This document is the map a reader in any of those venues can use to see where a component sits before reading it.

Security considerations

Each component specification carries its own security analysis, which this document does not duplicate: [[AGENT-AUTHZ]] (mid-chain key compromise, status availability, stale replay versus the evaluator's clock, issuer–key binding, layer conflation); [[ODRL-DELEGATION]] (acyclicity, depth budgets, the profile's deny-biased restriction of the use-implies-grantUse hierarchy, revocation trust rules); [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]] (existence non-disclosure, pre-evaluation dataset restriction); [[VC-DATA-INTEGRITY]] and [[RFC9449]] for the cryptographic substrate; and [[AAR-DESIGN]] §5 for the composed runtime's enforced-versus-accountable boundary.

The concerns that arise only from the composition deserve statement here:

  • Layer conflation is the composition's characteristic failure. A verified credential is not an authorization; a permitted policy chain with unverified signatures is not authority; a valid chain presented by a party other than its leaf delegate is not that party's authority. The four-phase order of [[AGENT-AUTHZ]], ending in the fail-closed evaluation of [[ODRL-DELEGATION]] and anchored to the transport-authenticated WebID (L1), exists precisely so no layer's "yes" is mistaken for the stack's.
  • Trust anchoring precedes everything. The chain evaluator decides validity given an authenticated chain; a verifier must establish that the root grant is genuinely the resource owner's (e.g. fetched from a location the owner controls) before any later phase means anything.
  • Unavailability resolves closed, everywhere. Unreachable status lists, unresolvable keys, unverifiable dates, missing grants: at every layer the composed answer to "cannot determine" is deny. A composition is only as fail-closed as its most fail-open member.
  • The natural-language edge is an injection surface. The NL channel exists to negotiate protocol documents, not to carry authority; the no-silent-downgrade rule (L5) and the rule that mandates are only ever the signed L3/L4 artifacts — never prose — keep prompt-injection outside the authority path.
  • Discovery touches attacker-controlled origins. Agent cards, protocol documents, revocation lists, and profile documents are fetched from URLs found in third-party data; implementations need standard server-side request-forgery defences on those fetches (as the reference implementations apply).

Privacy considerations

Again by reference first: [[AGENT-AUTHZ]] and [[VC-DATA-MODEL-2.0]] on credential correlation; [[SOLID-SPARQL-QUERY]] on query-interface non-disclosure; [[WAC]]/[[ACP]] on access-control metadata exposure. Composition-level concerns:

  • The accountability trail is itself personal data. PROV records, delegation chains, and agreements reveal who delegated what to whom, when, and for what purpose. They are ordinary pod resources and MUST (per the component specs' own terms) sit under access control like any other resource; "auditable by the parties with standing" is an access-control policy decision, not a property of publishing the trail to the world.
  • Mandates are correlatable. An AgentAuthorizationCredential binds a principal's WebID to an agent's across every context where it is presented. Presenting the minimal chain (and, in future, selective disclosure through the pluggable proof-suite seam) limits the exposure; the architecture inherits, and awaits, the VC ecosystem's selective-disclosure maturation.
  • Discovery is advertisement. Publishing an agent descriptor from a WebID profile announces that the identity operates an agent, and what it can do. This is by design — discoverability is the feature — but the descriptor's placement under the owner's control (their profile, their pod) is what keeps opting out possible.
